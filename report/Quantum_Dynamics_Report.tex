\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Simulating interacting quantum many-body systems is computationally expensive on classical computers: the state space grows exponentially as $2^N$, and quantum entanglement creates complex spatial and temporal correlations. In this project, we use deep learning sequence models to learn and forecast qubit dynamics from short observation windows. We study the $N = 10$ qubit PXP Rydberg-blockade Hamiltonian across $D = 55$ continuous physical observables ($10$ magnetizations and $45$ pairwise correlations) over $400$ simulated trajectories. Following an incremental workflow, we first evaluate a pilot study on $20$ trajectories and test dimensionality reduction via an Autoencoder ($55 \to 16 \to 55$), showing that operating directly on native observables avoids an irreducible reconstruction bottleneck. We then implement and benchmark four sequence architectures across Recurrent (M1, M2) and Transformer (M3, M4) families, trained with a unified multi-phase loop (Teacher Forcing, Masked Modeling, Scheduled Sampling, Full Autoregressive). In our systematic benchmark, the one-shot Transformer \textbf{M3 (HP3)} achieves the best performance ($\text{Test MSE} = 1.21 \times 10^{-4}$, $\text{MAE} = 0.00785$), improving by $25\%$ over the best RNN \textbf{M1} ($1.61 \times 10^{-4}$) with zero violations of quantum density-matrix positivity ($P(s_i, s_j) \ge 0$). Finally, we resolve all three project challenges: (1) an aligned window analysis shows that an input window of $T_{\text{in}} = 10$ steps is sufficient for accurate multi-step forecasting; (2) temporal super-resolution ($\times 2$) reconstructs fine-grained future steps near-losslessly from subsampled inputs; and (3) decoder attention maps confirm causal masking ($\max_{j>i}|\alpha_{ij}| = 0$) and reveal physical specialization between boundary anchoring and quantum-scar periodicity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Quantum computing is a promising approach for modeling physical phenomena, but studying and simulating many interacting qubits on classical computers is difficult. The primary obstacle is that the state space grows exponentially ($2^N$), causing exact numerical simulation to scale poorly with system size and time horizon. Beyond a modest number of qubits, direct calculation becomes intractable, and the quantum system effectively behaves as a black box.

Data-driven deep learning methods offer a powerful alternative~\cite{carleo2017}. By training sequence models on observable trajectories from tractable regimes, neural networks can learn the underlying dynamical rules directly from data and predict future states without computing the full exponential state vector. In this project, we study the $N = 10$ qubit \textbf{PXP model}~\cite{bernien2017}, which describes a 1D chain of Rydberg atoms with nearest-neighbor excitation blockade. Under this Hamiltonian, the system exhibits long-lived non-thermalizing periodic revivals known as \emph{quantum many-body scars}.

At each time step, the state of the system is monitored via $D = 55$ continuous expectation values: $10$ single-qubit magnetizations and $45$ pairwise two-body correlations. In addition to comparing Recurrent Neural Networks (LSTM/GRU)~\cite{sutskever2014} and Attention-based Transformers~\cite{vaswani2017}, this report tackles three official challenges:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Challenge 1 --- Minimizing the Input Window:} Finding the shortest historical observation window $T_{\text{in}} \in \{5, 10, 20, 40\}$ that yields accurate forecasts over a fixed horizon ($T_{\text{out}} = 40$), evaluated on strictly aligned targets.
    \item \textbf{Challenge 2 --- Temporal Super-Resolution ($\times 2$):} Reconstructing fine-grained future dynamics ($\Delta \tau = 0.02$) from a $2\times$ subsampled observation ($\Delta \tau_{\text{sub}} = 0.04$), formulated as a sequence imputation task.
    \item \textbf{Challenge 3 --- Interpretability via Attention Maps:} Inspecting decoder attention maps to discover how the network attends to past quantum oscillations and verify causal masking.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section~2 describes data verification, normalization, and windowing. Section~3 details model architectures, the preliminary pilot study, and training strategies. Section~4 presents the experimental benchmark, solutions to the three challenges, and error analyses. Section~5 discusses future work, followed by conclusions in Section~6.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset}
The dataset contains $N_{\text{traj}} = 400$ independent trajectories generated from the 10-qubit PXP model. Each trajectory spans $T = 1001$ discrete time steps sampled at uniform intervals $\Delta \tau = 0.02$ up to $\tau_{\text{fin}} = 20.0$, yielding $400,400$ total steps. Time is expressed in natural dimensionless units $\tau = t\Omega$ (with $\hbar \equiv 1$), where $\Omega$ is the characteristic Rabi coupling frequency.

Each time step provides $D = 55$ continuous physical observables:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}(\tau) = \bigl[\, m_1(\tau), \dots, m_{10}(\tau), \; c_{1,2}(\tau), \dots, c_{9,10}(\tau) \,\bigr]^\top \in [-1, 1]^{55},
\end{equation}
where $m_i(\tau) = \langle \sigma_z^i(\tau) \rangle$ is the local Pauli-$Z$ magnetization of qubit $i$, and $c_{ij}(\tau) = \langle \sigma_z^i(\tau)\sigma_z^j(\tau) \rangle$ ($1 \le i < j \le 10$) is the spatial two-body correlation. The dataset is complete with zero missing values.

\textbf{Quantum Physical Sanity Checks.} Before constructing sliding windows, we verified two fundamental quantum mechanical properties across all $400,400$ time steps:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Two-Body Density Matrix Positivity:} For every pair of qubits $(i, j)$ and possible spin configurations $s_i, s_j \in \{-1, +1\}$, the joint measurement probability must remain non-negative:
    \begin{equation}
        P(s_i, s_j) = \frac{1 + s_i m_i + s_j m_j + s_i s_j c_{ij}}{4} \ge 0.
        \label{eq:quantum_positivity}
    \end{equation}
    Across all $72,072,000$ evaluations, the minimum observed probability is $P_{\min} = 0.1553 > 0$, confirming exact physical validity ($0\%$ violations) and consistent observable ordering.
    \item \textbf{Initial Product-State Factorization:} At $\tau = 0$, trajectories initiate near unentangled product states where $c_{ij}(0) \approx m_i(0)m_j(0)$. We observe a mean absolute difference $|c_{ij}(0) - m_i(0)m_j(0)| = 0.0158$, consistent with unentangled initialization.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Figure~\ref{fig:data_and_norm}a illustrates sample trajectories of magnetizations and correlations. To prepare the raw sequences for model training, we apply a two-step preprocessing workflow: trajectory-level splitting and normalization.

\textbf{Trajectory Splitting.} The $400$ trajectories are split at the trajectory level into $70\%$ training ($280$ trajectories), $15\%$ validation ($60$ trajectories), and $15\%$ test ($60$ trajectories). Splitting whole trajectories rather than slicing time windows across trajectories is essential to prevent temporal data leakage.

\textbf{Normalization Strategy.} Because all $55$ observables are Pauli spin expectation values, they are strictly bounded within $[-1, 1]$. We conducted an empirical ablation comparing raw physical inputs paired with a bounded $\tanh$ output activation against column-wise Z-score standardization paired with a linear head (Table~\ref{tab:norm_ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:data_and_norm}b).

\textbf{Note.} Retaining raw physical bounds with a $\tanh$ head yields lower test error ($1.24 \times 10^{-4}$ vs.\ $1.42 \times 10^{-4}$) and mathematically guarantees that network predictions never exceed physical bounds $[-1, 1]$, completely eliminating out-of-bounds artifacts.

\textbf{Sliding Window Generation.} We extract temporal windows using a sliding stride $s$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Forecasting:} Given input length $T_{\text{in}}$ and forecasting horizon $T_{\text{out}}$, each sample forms an input tensor $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{in}} \times D}$ and target tensor $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T_{\text{out}} \times D}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:windowing}a).
    \item \textbf{Super-Resolution:} For window length $W$, the input contains an extra binary mask channel: $\mathbf{X}_{\text{sr}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times W \times (D+1)}$, and the model reconstructs the full dense sequence $\mathbf{Y}_{\text{sr}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times W \times D}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Models Overview}
To ensure methodological rigor, we adopted a staged development process:

\textbf{Preliminary Pilot Study.} Before running large-scale training, we implemented a pilot study on $N_{\text{pilot}} = 20$ trajectories ($19{,}220$ windows) to verify pipeline throughput, memory constraints, and loss convergence (Fig.~\ref{fig:start_small_ae}a).

\textbf{Autoencoder Dimensionality Reduction.} We investigated whether compressing the $55$ observables into a low-dimensional latent space ($55 \to 16 \to 55$) could benefit sequence learning. Although the Autoencoder reconstructs individual time frames with high accuracy (MSE $= 2.01 \times 10^{-4}$), training sequence models in latent space compounds errors: latent-space forecasting yielded an end-to-end MSE of $5.79 \times 10^{-4}$, nearly four times worse than training directly in native space ($1.21 \times 10^{-4}$, Fig.~\ref{fig:start_small_ae}b). Consequently, all primary forecasting models operate directly on native observables ($D = 55$).

\subsection{Model Architectures}
\textbf{Notation:} $B$ = batch size, $T_{\text{in}}$ = input sequence length, $T_{\text{out}}$ = forecasting horizon, $T_{\text{dec}}$ = decoder sequence length, $D = 55$ = feature dimension, $d_{\text{lat}}$ = latent dimension, $d_{\text{model}}$ = model dimension, $d_{\text{ff}}$ = feed-forward dimension, $H$ = attention heads, $W$ = window size, $N$ = stacked layers. All configurations are governed by modular YAML files.

We implemented four forecasting sequence models across two architectural families:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{M1 --- One-Shot RNN (Full-Seq):} Encoder-decoder LSTM. The encoder consumes the input sequence; the final hidden state initializes the decoder, which directly projects the full output horizon in one pass.
    \item \textbf{M2 --- Autoregressive Seq2Seq RNN (Hybrid):} Encoder-decoder LSTM that generates predictions step-by-step, feeding each generated prediction back as the next input step.
    \item \textbf{M3 --- One-Shot Direct Transformer:} Encoder-only Transformer. Input steps are encoded with multi-head self-attention, flattened, and projected to the full future horizon via a multi-layer perceptron.
    \item \textbf{M4 --- Causal Seq2Seq Transformer (Hybrid):} Full Encoder-Decoder Transformer with causal masking in self-attention and cross-attention attending to encoder representations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Strategies}
\textbf{Important.} The training strategies described in this subsection apply to iterative sequence forecasting models; super-resolution uses standard masked reconstruction.

    \label{tab:yaml_options}
    \begin{tabular}{ll}

        \textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Custom Options Exposed in YAML} \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Note.} The learning rate and gradient clipping threshold are defined globally and scaled per-phase using multipliers (\texttt{lr\_multiplier}, \texttt{clip\_norm\_multiplier}) to ensure smooth transition dynamics between phases.

\textbf{Curriculum Learning.} We train autoregressive models using a phase-wise curriculum~\cite{bengio2009}. Instead of computing the loss over the entire output horizon from epoch 1, at phase $k$ the loss is backpropagated only on an effective horizon $H_k = c_k \cdot T_{\text{out}}$, where $c_k$ is the $k$-th entry of \texttt{curriculum}.

\textbf{Example.} If $T_{\text{out}} = 40$ and \texttt{curriculum} $= [0.25, 0.50, 1.0]$, the model is optimized first on $H_1 = 10$ steps, then on $H_2 = 20$ steps, and finally on the full $H_3 = 40$ steps.

\textbf{Prediction Mode (\texttt{all} vs.\ \texttt{horizon}).} To support variable-horizon curriculum learning in TensorFlow/Keras without incurring repeated graph retracing overhead~\cite{tf2024}, we support two prediction modes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \texttt{all}: The model always predicts the full $T_{\text{out}}$, but the loss is sliced and backpropagated only up to $H_k$. This preserves static tensor shapes and prevents graph recompilation.
    \item \texttt{horizon}: The model dynamically decodes exactly $H_k$ steps.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Free-Running Evaluation and Probes}
During training, loss values computed with teacher forcing can be overly optimistic because the model receives ground-truth inputs at every step. To assess true inference performance, we execute periodic \textbf{free-running probes}~\cite{bengio2015} using an unassisted inference loop.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
Hyperparameter tuning was conducted across two systematic levels:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Architecture and Capacity):} For each of the four model families, we evaluated three representative capacity configurations (HP1: compact, HP2: baseline, HP3: deep/high-capacity) across learning rate, clipping norm, hidden dimensions, and attention heads.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Windowing and Horizons):} We explored windowing configurations over input lengths $T_{\text{in}} \in \{5, 10, 20, 40\}$ under a fixed forecasting horizon $T_{\text{out}} = 40$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Challenge 1: Minimizing the Input Window}
To determine the minimal history required to predict quantum dynamics, we evaluated four input window lengths: $T_{\text{in}} \in \{5, 10, 20, 40\}$ (corresponding to physical observation durations $\tau_{\text{obs}} \in [0.10, 0.80]$).

\textbf{Fair Comparison via Strictly Aligned Targets.} When testing different $T_{\text{in}}$, a naive sliding window predicts different absolute time slices ($[T_{\text{in}}, T_{\text{in}}+T_{\text{out}}]$), which biases the comparison because early quantum steps are easier to predict than later steps. To guarantee a strictly fair comparison, we implemented an aligned evaluation: the target window is fixed at $[40, 80]$ for all models, and shorter inputs use only the final $T_{\text{in}}$ steps of the historical window $[40-T_{\text{in}}, 40]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:challenge1_aligned}).

\subsection{Challenge 2: Temporal Super-Resolution}
In experimental Rydberg simulators, measurement rates can be constrained by camera readout speeds or laser switching limits. Challenge 2 evaluates whether a network can reconstruct fine-grained dynamics ($\Delta \tau = 0.02$) when historical inputs are subsampled by $2\times$ ($\Delta \tau_{\text{sub}} = 0.04$).

We formulate super-resolution as a masked sequence imputation task. Subsampled positions are interleaved with a masking token, and an extra binary channel ($D+1$) flags missing indices.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:challenge2_super_res}, the model reconstructs the intermediate unobserved states near-losslessly, capturing both turning points and inflection curvature.

\subsection{Challenge 3: Interpretability via Attention Maps}
To understand how sequence models capture quantum dynamics, we extracted decoder self-attention and cross-attention matrices from the Causal Transformer (M4).

\textbf{Decoder Causal Self-Attention.} Figure~\ref{fig:challenge3_attention}b verifies the causal mask. Because future tokens must never leak information backward in time during training, upper-triangular attention weights must be strictly zero. Numerical inspection confirms:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{j > i} |\alpha_{ij}| = 0.0 \quad (\text{exact zero}), \qquad \sum_{j \le i} \alpha_{ij} = 1.0 \pm 1.79 \times 10^{-7},
\end{equation}
confirming exact compliance with the physical arrow of time.

\section{Future Work}
% =============================================================================

Our current model selection focused on optimizing validation score for a fixed forecasting horizon $T_{\text{out}} = 40$. In future work, this methodology can be extended in three key directions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Three-Dimensional Pareto Front:} Windowing hyperparameters create natural trade-offs between accuracy, input context length, and prediction horizon. Future work can construct a 3D Pareto front optimizing simultaneously: (i) minimize prediction MSE, (ii) maximize forecasting horizon $T_{\text{out}}$, and (iii) minimize input history $T_{\text{in}}$. This would identify the globally optimal knee-point configuration under explicit memory and latency constraints.
    \item \textbf{Mitigating Autoregressive Exposure Bias:} While one-shot models (M3) avoid exposure bias, causal decoder architectures (M4) offer superior interpretability. Exploring curriculum-paced scheduled sampling with reinforcement-style reward shaping or adversarial rollouts could bridge the performance gap between one-shot and autoregressive decoding over very long horizons.
    \item \textbf{Scaling to Larger Qubit Registers ($N > 10$):} Scaling from $10$ qubits to $N = 50$ or $100$ atoms increases the correlation space quadratically ($D \sim N^2 / 2$). Combining spatial graph neural networks (GNNs) with temporal transformers could exploit the 1D lattice topology to scale predictions efficiently to larger experimental arrays.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

In this work, we developed and systematically benchmarked deep sequence models for predicting the quantum many-body dynamics of a 10-qubit Rydberg PXP chain across $55$ continuous observables. Our key findings are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Native Observables vs.\ Autoencoders:} Operating directly on raw bounded observables ($[-1, 1]$ with $\tanh$ head) avoids an irreducible information bottleneck ($5.79 \times 10^{-4}$ in latent space vs.\ $1.21 \times 10^{-4}$ native) and guarantees exact physical validity with zero positivity violations ($P(s_i, s_j) \ge 0$).
    \item \textbf{Architecture Benchmark:} The One-Shot Transformer \textbf{M3 (HP3)} achieves the best performance (Test MSE $= 1.21 \times 10^{-4}$, MAE $= 0.0078$), outperforming the best RNN \textbf{M1} by $25\%$ while training $2.5\times$ faster per epoch due to parallel sequence attention.
    \item \textbf{Challenge Solutions:} We resolved all three project challenges:
    \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
        \item \emph{Challenge 1:} Under strictly aligned target evaluation, $T_{\text{in}} = 10$ steps ($\tau_{\text{obs}} = 0.20$) achieves $95\%$ of the accuracy of $T_{\text{in}} = 40$ using only one quarter of the input history.
        \item \emph{Challenge 2:} Temporal super-resolution ($\times 2$) reconstructs dense trajectories near-losslessly (MSE $= 1.32 \times 10^{-4}$) from $2\times$ subsampled inputs.
        \item \emph{Challenge 3:} Decoder attention maps confirm strict causal lower-triangular masking ($\max_{j>i}|\alpha_{ij}| = 0$) and demonstrate physical head specialization between boundary phase-locking and quantum-scar periodicity.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
